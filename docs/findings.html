Finding

Three quarters of everything that claims a machine interface serves a web page

A registration file can declare a services[] endpoint. Declaring one is free, and nothing checks it. So we split the question in two and kept the answers in separate columns: declared is what the operator wrote, verified is what the endpoint did when called.

An indexer that reports declarations reports 6,511 callable agents. Verification finds 177. The ratio is the point rather than either absolute number: both sides grow as resolution completes, and no amount of care in a ranking function closes the gap, because the error sits upstream of the ranking.

Not final — and which way it moves

49,366 http-hosted registrations are still unfetched: 15.4% of the corpus. Until they resolve they sit in none by default, so every class count on this page understates what the registry declares. 22,882 of them failed transiently while two of our own fetcher processes competed for the same hosts; refetching those by hand afterwards returned HTTP 200.

The direction matters more than the figure. Unresolved registrations can only add declared endpoints, so the verified count is a floor and the deadness of this registry is overstated here rather than understated — which is the direction that flatters our own argument. That is why it sits at the top of the page instead of in a footnote. The reputation figures in the next two sections come from a complete scan and are final.

The consequence is not subtle. Any marketplace that ranks agents on this registry's feedback is ranking on four opinions. The registry permits it: giveFeedback has no authorisation gate, so any address may rate any agent with no evidence of ever having transacted with it. That is a property of the standard, not a bug in anyone's deployment — but a marketplace that surfaces the resulting score as a rating has passed it along without checking who wrote it.

We publish the composition instead of a score: how many distinct raters, what share the largest one holds, and who they are.

Finding

Concentration alone would have called the rings healthy

Rater concentration catches the crude case immediately — one address, forty ratings. It does not catch the careful one. Sixty agents carry twenty to twenty-nine distinct raters with no single rater holding more than 15% of the ratings. By that measure they look better than almost everything else on the registry.

So we asked a different question: who else do these raters rate?

Closure is the share of an agent's raters that also rate one single other agent. At 100%, every one of those twenty-eight independent-looking raters rates the same other agent — and each of them rates around 132 agents in total. Across the whole registry, 4,371 of 4,401 rated agents sit at 90% closure or above. Twenty sit below 10%.

Two signals, opposite verdicts, same data. We ship both and show the disagreement rather than resolving it into a number.

Counterweight

What this does not prove

A closed rater graph is consistent with coordinated rating. It is also consistent with a single platform operating many agents and rating its own fleet for internal bookkeeping, which is neither secret nor fraudulent. We cannot tell those apart from on-chain data, and the signal does not claim to. It reports structure; it does not allege intent, and the interface must not either.

Nor is a dead endpoint proof of a dead agent. An agent may be perfectly functional and reachable through a channel it never declared. What we can say is narrow and exact: this is what the registration claims, and this is what happened when we called it.

The 23 agents pointing at testnet are the clearest case for humility. That is a deployment mistake by someone who built a working agent, not an abandoned identity, and it is fixable in a minute by whoever owns it. We hold it in its own class rather than filing it under dead.

Correction

Two things we got wrong, and the direction they failed in

Correction 1 — liquidity

We reported that the escrow token had no market, having checked the PancakeSwap V2 pair and found roughly one and a half cents of depth. On BSC, stablecoin liquidity lives in V3 0.01% pools. The real pool holds about 11.0M U against 10.0M USDT. The V2 pair is an abandoned shell. This nearly justified building our own escrow contract.

Correction 2 — "nothing here is hireable"

We twice concluded the registry contained zero live task interfaces. Both times the denominator was wrong: the http-hosted half of the corpus — 146,461 registrations — had not been fetched, and that is precisely where the working platforms turned out to live. The counts were accurate; the claim built on them was not.

Both errors failed in the same direction: toward our own thesis. A registry that looks deader makes a filter that subtracts look smarter. That is the bias this project has to be most careful about, because it is the one nobody on the team is motivated to go looking for.

The rule we adopted in response: a transport failure must never be recordable in a shape indistinguishable from an on-chain fact. A contract reverting is data. A request timing out is not. Where the two collapse into one null, the corpus quietly shrinks and every scarcity figure improves.

Method

How to recount every number here

Both of our errors failed toward the thesis, and the same pressure sits on this document. So none of it should be taken on trust.

Enumeration, not sampling

The registry is an ERC-721 without Enumerable — totalSupply() reverts — so agents cannot be listed on chain. We walk every token id from 1 upward by eth_call, which needs no archive node, no log history and no third-party index. An unminted id reverts with ERC721NonexistentToken; that revert is treated as a fact, while a node error is treated as our failure and retried without advancing the cursor.

  "tokenURI(uint256)(string)" 262517 --rpc-url https://bsc-rpc.publicnode.com

Rater graph

Feedback concentration and closure come from getClients(agentId) and getLastIndex(agentId, client) — view functions, so the whole graph is readable without indexing a single log. Note that getSummary reverts on an empty client array by design: the standard has no global score, which is exactly why ranking is a product decision rather than a lookup.

Classification

The classifier is pinned by a golden test over 300 real agents captured from the live registry, with their registration files as fetched. It asserts the class distribution, the operator concentration, and that the malformed tail — hackathon config blobs, a free-text advert, an agent whose tokenURI is the two-character string "" — classifies without throwing. If a change to the classifier moves those counts, the test fails and the change has to be argued rather than absorbed.

Three claims, three levels of confidence

They are not interchangeable, and collapsing them into a single "full sweep" is the mistake this page was corrected for making:

  • Identity enumeration is complete. 321,016 ids, every one read directly. Final.
  • Registration resolution stands at 84.6%. 49,366 http-hosted files remain, so every declaredClass count here is provisional and will grow.
  • The verified count is a floor. 177 today; unresolved registrations can only add to it.

One operator's registrations sit at 48.9% and will stay there. Finishing would take about five hours of sustained requests against a host already returning 429, to sharpen a proportion already measured across more than half its population. We stopped, and say so, rather than reporting a number as complete when it is not.
